# Camera Movement Director

Translate dramatic intent into motivated, executable camera direction.

## Timing in scene design

Use this skill proactively when designing a film, animation, or generated-video scene; the user need not request camera advice explicitly.

1. Establish the story beat, audience knowledge, emotional intent, and rough actor or subject blocking.
2. Before finalizing the storyboard, shot list, animatic, or generation prompt, run the camera movement pass below.
3. Carry its decisions into those downstream artifacts. Do not leave camera direction as a separate optional appendix.
4. Revisit affected shots when subject paths, timing, location, lens, framing, or equipment changes.
5. Review continuity and practical feasibility before delivering the final scene plan.

For an early concept, keep the pass lightweight and provisional. For a locked scene, give executable instructions. Do not force camera planning into unrelated writing, still photography, or scenes that are not intended for visual production.

## Principles

- Choose the storytelling purpose before the movement. Consider stillness.
- Treat emotional associations as contextual, not guaranteed effects.
- Separate camera motion from subject motion, support equipment, angle, and shot size.
- Specify start, cue, path, timing, and end. Avoid decorative movement without purpose.
- Use existing context; state reasonable assumptions. Ask only about material unknowns.
- Never claim to have inspected footage or transcripts that were not accessible.

## Camera movement pass

### Establish the brief

Identify action, dramatic beat, intended audience experience, medium, duration, aspect ratio, location, subject paths, available support, and any required start/end frames. For AI video, use the named platform's actual capabilities when known; verify current platform-specific controls before relying on them.

### Define the purpose

Choose a concrete purpose: direct attention, reveal information, explain geography, accompany action, change emotional proximity, express subjectivity, show scale, or shape rhythm. State what changes for the audience and which story beat motivates that change.

### Choose and describe the move

| Term | Physical meaning |
|---|---|
| Static | Camera position and orientation remain fixed. |
| Pan | Rotate left/right from a fixed position. |
| Tilt | Rotate up/down from a fixed position. |
| Roll | Rotate around the viewing axis. |
| Push-in | Travel toward the subject. |
| Pull-out | Travel away from the subject. |
| Truck | Translate sideways. |
| Pedestal | Translate vertically. |
| Tracking | Travel through the scene, often accompanying a subject. |
| Arc/orbit | Travel on a curved path around a subject. |
| Boom/crane | A supporting arm carries the camera through an elevated or sweeping path. |
| Zoom | Change lens focal length; travel is not required. |
| Dolly zoom | Combine travel and an opposing zoom to approximately preserve subject size while changing background appearance. |
| Whip pan | Rotate rapidly left/right, typically producing motion blur. |

Handheld, gimbal, Steadicam, dolly, and drone describe support or operating methods. An overhead view is an angle, a Dutch angle is an orientation, and a long take describes duration/editing. None alone specifies a path.

Pan versus truck: rotation versus translation; translation produces parallax. Tilt versus pedestal: orientation versus height. Push-in versus zoom: changing viewpoint versus changing focal length from a viewpoint. Roll versus Dutch angle: rotation during the shot versus a tilted composition that may remain still.

Specify starting composition and angle, subject action, camera path, starting cue, speed/acceleration, focus behavior, ending composition and stopping cue, duration, and purpose. Prefer one clear primary move; combine moves when the story requires it.

### Check feasibility

Check path clearance, support and operator requirements, focus distance, lighting/exposure changes, occlusion, reflections, horizon, unwanted equipment in frame, screen direction, spatial clarity, and edit handles. If crossing the action axis, make geography understandable. Stabilization does not solve weak composition or all footstep bounce. Flag real rigging or flight constraints only when relevant to the proposed shot.

### Deliver

For one shot:

- Purpose
- Camera direction: start → cue → path → end
- Timing and speed
- Execution: support, focus, essential constraints
- Most likely failure and correction
- One simpler alternative when useful

For a scene:

| Shot | Action/beat | Start → end | Movement and cue | Duration | Purpose | Execution |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Integrate the result into the user's requested artifact. Use only fields that help. When duration is approximate, label it; make shot durations consistent with the scene's runtime.

## AI video prompts

Write visible behavior: starting composition + subject action + camera path + speed/duration + ending composition + essential continuity constraints. Keep subject motion distinct from camera motion. Avoid contradictory directions, excessive moves in short clips, and vague instructions such as “make it cinematic.” Do not promise exact execution by a generative system or invent platform controls.

Example (invented): Begin at eye level in a medium shot of a woman seated at a kitchen table. As she opens a letter, slowly move the camera straight toward her over approximately six seconds, ending in a close-up. She remains seated; maintain a level horizon.

## Review and teaching

Review motivation, terminology, geography, timing, framing, focus, exposure, feasibility, and editability. Lead with the most consequential issue, explain its effect, and give a corrected direction that preserves creative intent.

When teaching, explain physical motion, image change, a contextual story use, a practical exercise, and a common mistake. Label invented examples. Do not invent source timestamps, quotations, or film examples.
